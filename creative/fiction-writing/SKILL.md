## When to Use
Write short stories, novels, screenplays, or narrative content. Use when developing characters, building worlds, crafting dialogue, structuring plot arcs, or overcoming writer's block.

## Core Concepts
- **Story structure**: Three-act (setup/confrontation/resolution), Hero's Journey (12 stages), Save the Cat (beat sheet), Dan Harmon's Story Circle.
- **Character development**: Characters want something (desire), face obstacles (conflict), and are changed by the experience (arc). Flat characters lack desire or change.
- **Show don't tell**: "She was angry" (tell) vs "She slammed the cabinet door hard enough to rattle the dishes" (show).
- **Dialogue subtext**: Characters rarely say exactly what they mean. The tension between what's said and what's meant creates interest.
- **Scene vs Summary**: Scenes happen in real-time with dialogue and action. Summary compresses time. Use scenes at turning points.
- **Voice**: The distinct personality of the narration. Consistent voice is what makes a writer recognizable.

## Workflow
1. **Concept**: One sentence premise. "A washed-up detective must solve a murder in a town where everyone is a suspect — including himself."
2. **Outline**: Map the major plot points. Know your ending before you write the beginning.
3. **Character profiles**: For main characters: desire, fear, flaw, secret, voice. What do they want vs what they need?
4. **Scene list**: Each scene must advance plot or reveal character (ideally both). Cut scenes that don't.
5. **First draft**: Write forward. Don't edit as you go. Speed matters more than quality in the first draft.
6. **Revision pass 1**: Structure. Does the plot work? Are there gaps? Is the pacing right?
7. **Revision pass 2**: Character. Are motivations clear? Does dialogue sound distinct per character?
8. **Revision pass 3**: Language. Cut adverbs, tighten prose, eliminate clichés.

10. Bad Guys Close In (50-75%): Antagonist escalates
11. All Is Lost (75%): Hero's lowest moment
12. Dark Night of the Soul (75-80%): Hero processes the loss
13. Break into Three (80%): Aha moment — theme internalized
14. Finale (80-99%): Hero applies new understanding
15. Final Image (100%): Mirror of opening — shows transformation

# Dialogue Tips
- Read dialogue aloud. If it sounds stiff, it is.
- Each character should have a distinct verbal fingerprint (one uses contractions, one doesn't)
- Cut dialogue tags when the speaker is obvious: "I don't think so," she said → "I don't think so."
- Subtext: What characters DON'T say matters more than what they do

## Pitfalls
- **Passive protagonists**: Characters to whom things happen, rather than characters who make things happen
- **Info-dumping**: Explaining backstory in blocks. Reveal it through action, dialogue, and detail.
- **Perfect characters**: Flaws make characters human. If the reader can't relate to the struggle, they won't care about the victory
- **Deus ex machina**: Problems solved by coincidence. Characters must earn their victories
- **Purple prose**: Overwriting draws attention to the writing, not the story. Simple is powerful.
- **No stakes**: If nothing is at risk, why should the reader care? Raise the stakes.

## Verification
- First page test: Does the opening page make you want to read page 2?
- Beta readers: 3-5 people who read the full draft and provide honest feedback
- Pacing check: Mark each scene as "advances plot" or "reveals character" — if neither, cut it
- Dialogue test: Cover character names — can you tell who's speaking from voice alone?
- Read aloud: Catch awkward phrasing and unnatural rhythm
